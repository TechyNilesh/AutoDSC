\begin{abstract}
We introduce \textit{AutoDSC} (\underline{Auto}mated \underline{D}ata \underline{S}tream \underline{C}lustering), a novel AutoML framework that automatically selects and tunes clustering algorithms for evolving data streams. This matters because in IoT, network monitoring, and sensor applications, the data distribution changes continuously, so no fixed algorithm or configuration stays optimal, yet clustering lacks a universal objective to optimise, which is why AutoML has transformed supervised learning, yet its extension to unsupervised streams remains largely unexplored. \textit{AutoDSC} achieves fully autonomous adaptivity by continuously searching the joint algorithm and hyperparameter space using population-guided stochastic search optimisation and selecting the best configuration at each window stage using a multi-objective quality measure, without ground-truth labels. We benchmark AutoDSC against state-of-the-art stream-clustering and AutoML methods on ten representative real-world and synthetic data streams. AutoDSC attains the best overall rank in clustering quality among all compared methods while remaining computationally efficient.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Data stream clustering is a fundamental operation across a wide range of real-world applications, from IoT sensor networks and network intrusion detection to social media analysis and financial monitoring~\cite{yang2022iot,bahri2021data}. Unlike batch clustering, stream clustering must process data continuously, adapting to distributions that change over time. Numerous stream clustering algorithms have been proposed~\cite{aggarwal2003framework,Cao2006,Hahsler2016,montiel2021river}, each effective under specific conditions. However, the right algorithm and its hyperparameters must themselves change as the stream evolves, since no single configuration stays optimal throughout. Existing automated solutions either rely on a single evaluation metric~\cite{carnein2020confstream}, require computationally intensive offline tuning~\cite{el2023onlineautoclust}, or reconfigure structural components without jointly searching the algorithm and hyperparameter space~\cite{wang2024mostream}, leaving a clear gap for a fully autonomous, label-free AutoML approach to stream clustering.

In this paper, we introduce \textit{AutoDSC} (Automated Data Stream Clustering), a novel AutoML framework that automatically selects and tunes a clustering algorithm online as the data distribution evolves. We make the following key contributions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item We formalise data stream clustering as a combined algorithm selection and hyperparameter optimisation (CASH) problem~\cite{hutter2019automated} that must be solved continuously as the data distribution evolves, and present \textit{AutoDSC}, a novel AutoML framework that solves it autonomously without manual tuning or labels.
    \item \textit{AutoDSC} introduces Population-Guided Stochastic Search Optimisation (PGSSO), which maintains a recency-weighted elite archive and an adaptively controlled exploration rate so that promising configurations are identified quickly and the population recovers implicitly when the distribution shifts.
    \item \textit{AutoDSC} scores each configuration with a normalised multi-objective quality measure combining multiple internal cluster-validity indices, yielding robust selection.
    \item We provide a comprehensive empirical evaluation across ten real-world and synthetic streams, demonstrating that \textit{AutoDSC} consistently ranks best in clustering quality and is the most computationally efficient among the data stream clustering AutoML frameworks\footnote{Datasets and code: \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/AutoDSC}}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Data Stream Clustering}

A data stream is an unbounded, high-velocity sequence of instances that a learner must process continuously. A stream learner must satisfy five requirements~\cite{bifet2018machine}, namely to \textbf{RQ1}~process one instance at a time and inspect it at most once; \textbf{RQ2}~use a bounded amount of time per instance; \textbf{RQ3}~use a bounded amount of memory; \textbf{RQ4}~be ready to produce an answer at any time; and \textbf{RQ5}~adapt to temporal changes in the data. Because the data-generating distribution evolves, streams exhibit \textit{concept drift}, meaning the number, shape, and location of clusters changes over time, whether abruptly, gradually, or incrementally~\cite{bifet2018machine,aggarwal2007data}, meaning a static configuration that performs well today may become suboptimal as conditions shift. Classical stream clustering algorithms address RQ1--RQ5 and fall into families distinguished by how they summarise the stream: CluStream~\cite{aggarwal2003framework} maintains pyramidal time-frame statistics; density-based methods DenStream~\cite{Cao2006} and DBSTREAM~\cite{Hahsler2016} discover arbitrarily shaped clusters via core, potential, and outlier micro-clusters; and k-means-based methods maintain centroids online, exemplified by mini-batch web-scale clustering~\cite{Sculley2010} and StreamKMeans~\cite{montiel2021river}. Stream clustering quality is evaluated with the prequential (interleaved test-then-train) protocol~\cite{bifet2018machine}, in which each instance tests the current model before the model updates on it. Because ground-truth labels are unavailable during the stream, online quality assessment relies on \textit{internal} cluster validity indices (CVIs), computed from the data and cluster assignments alone: the Silhouette Coefficient~\cite{rousseeuw1987silhouettes} measures cohesion relative to neighbouring clusters; the Davies--Bouldin Index~\cite{davies1979cluster} measures the ratio of intra-cluster scatter to inter-cluster separation; and the Calinski--Harabasz Index~\cite{calinski1974dendrite} measures the ratio of between-cluster to within-cluster dispersion. \textit{External} indices require ground-truth labels for evaluation: the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)~\cite{hubert1985comparing,rand1971objective} is a chance-corrected measure of agreement between a predicted clustering and the true partition, yielding 1 for a perfect match and 0 for random assignment; and the Cluster Mapping Measure (CMM)~\cite{kremer2011cmm} extends external evaluation to the streaming setting by explicitly accounting for emerging, splitting, and moving clusters that classical external indices treat as errors.

\subsection{Automated Machine Learning}

AutoML formalises algorithm selection and hyperparameter optimisation as the Combined Algorithm Selection and Hyperparameter Optimisation (CASH) problem~\cite{thornton2013autoweka,feurer2015autosklearn,hutter2019automated}, with search strategies ranging from Bayesian optimisation and evolutionary methods to population-based search and random search~\cite{bergstra2012random,hansen2001cmaes}. Clustering-specific systems apply these strategies with meta-learning and Bayesian search strategies~\cite{Tschechlov2021AutoML4Clust,TrederTschechlov2023ML2DAC,ElShawi2021cSmartML,ElShawi2022TPEAutoClust}, but all assume full dataset access and a fixed distribution. AutoML has been extended to supervised data streams~\cite{pmlr-v139-wu21d,celik2023online,pmlr-v256-verma24a,verma2025auto-reg}, yet its extension to unsupervised streams, where no ground-truth labels are available to guide search, remains largely unexplored.

\subsection{Prior Work}\label{sec:relatedwork}

AutoML has been applied to batch clustering~\cite{Tschechlov2021AutoML4Clust,Liu2021AutoCluster,ElShawi2021cSmartML,TrederTschechlov2023ML2DAC,ElShawi2022TPEAutoClust,daSilva2024PoAC} and to supervised data streams~\cite{celik2023online,pmlr-v256-verma24a,pmlr-v139-wu21d,verma2025auto-reg}, yet no existing system jointly addresses the single-pass, label-free, and continuously drifting conditions of stream clustering. The closest prior work spans three systems. \textit{ConfStream}~\cite{carnein2020confstream} maintains an ensemble of configurations and resamples near the better-performing ones, but selects by a single Silhouette-Width score that proves brittle across datasets. \textit{OnlineAutoClust} (OACE)~\cite{el2023onlineautoclust} searches with Tree-structured Parzen Estimator Bayesian optimisation over clustering ensembles, but depends on a computationally heavy offline tuning budget and is slow to recover under rapid drift. \textit{MOStream}~\cite{wang2024mostream}, the automated successor of the modular Benne algorithm from the SESAME study~\cite{wang2023sesame}, reconfigures four structural design dimensions by rule rather than searching the joint algorithm-and-hyperparameter space. \textit{AutoDSC} addresses all this limitations through a single-pass population-guided search over the joint algorithm-and-hyperparameter space, selection by a multi-objective label-free quality measure, and implicit drift adaptation through continual re-evaluation.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

A data stream is an unbounded sequence $\mathcal{S} = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_t, \ldots\}$ in which each instance $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ arrives one at a time and is consumed in consecutive windows of fixed size $w$. Because ground-truth labels are unavailable online, clustering quality on window $W$ is judged by an internal cluster-validity score $q(\cdot) \in [0,1]$ computed from the data and assignments alone, while external indices are reserved for evaluation.

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a portfolio of stream-clustering algorithms

\begin{equation}
\Theta = \bigcup_{a \in \mathcal{A}} (\{a\} \times \Phi_a)
\end{equation}

the joint space of configurations $\theta = (a, \phi)$ pairing an algorithm $a$ with hyperparameters $\phi \in \Phi_a$. The CASH problem for a stream is to track, at the end of each stage, the configuration

\begin{equation}
\theta^\star = \arg\max_{\theta \in \Theta}\; q(\theta; W),
\end{equation}

using only the data seen so far and bounded memory. As the distribution drifts and $\theta^\star$ changes over time, so a solver must continually re-evaluate and re-select configurations.

\section{Automated Data Stream Clustering}\label{sec:methodology}

\textit{AutoDSC} addresses the online CASH problem of Section~\ref{sec:problem} as a novel AutoML framework tailored for non-stationary data stream clustering. \textit{AutoDSC} processes data in fixed-size stages, evaluating clustering configurations at the end of each stage using multiple normalised validity indices. The framework maintains a recency-weighted elite archive of high-performing configurations to guide future exploration via population-guided stochastic search. Figure~\ref{fig:framework_overview} illustrates the high-level workflow of \textit{AutoDSC}.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.70\linewidth]{images/diagrams/AutoDSC_Diagram.png}
    \caption{Overview of AutoDSC. At the end of each stage the population of clustering configurations is evaluated by the multi-objective metrics, the incumbent is re-selected, and PGSSO proposes new configurations from the recency-weighted elite archive under an adaptive exploration rate.}
    \label{fig:framework_overview}
\end{figure*}

The framework maintains a configuration population $\mathcal{A}_t \subseteq \Theta$ of clustering algorithms with different hyperparameter settings, fills the current window $W_t$ as instances arrive, and serves real-time predictions from the best-performing (incumbent) configuration. As each new data point arrives, every configuration in the population updates incrementally on it with a single \textsc{LearnOne} step, retaining the per-point cluster assignment for scoring. At the end of each window, \textit{AutoDSC} evaluates clustering quality using three normalised cluster validity indices, the Silhouette Coefficient (NSC), the Davies-Bouldin Index (NDBI), and the Calinski-Harabasz Index (NCHI). The expected clustering quality for configuration $\theta$ at time $t$ is

\begin{equation}
\mu_t(\theta) = \frac{1}{3}[\text{NSC} + \text{NDBI} + \text{NCHI}],
\end{equation}

where all indices are bounded to $[0,1]$ with higher values indicating better clustering quality. Equal weights avoid dataset-specific tuning and are consistent with the finding that no single CVI dominates across cluster shapes and densities~\cite{arbelaitz2013extensive,liu2010understanding}.

AutoDSC adjusts exploration based on the change in the best score between consecutive stages, $\Delta\mu_t = \mu_t(\theta^*_t) - \mu_{t-1}(\theta^*_{t-1})$. The exploration rate then adapts as:

\begin{equation}
\alpha_{t+1} = \begin{cases}
\max(0.1,\; \alpha_t \cdot (1 - \beta_t)) & \Delta \mu_t > 0 \\
\min(1.0,\; \alpha_t \cdot (1 + \beta_t)) & \Delta \mu_t \leq 0
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\beta_t = \min(\gamma,\; |r_t|)
\end{equation}

where $r_t = \Delta\mu_t / |\mu_{t-1}(\theta^*_{t-1})|$ is the relative improvement and $\beta_t$ is the resulting step, capped at $\gamma$ for stability. This parameter-free mechanism decreases exploration when performance improves and increases it when performance stagnates or drops, as happens under concept drift, encouraging broader search to recover.

The framework employs \textbf{Population-Guided Stochastic Search Optimisation (PGSSO)} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:pgsso}) to jointly optimise algorithm selection and hyperparameter tuning. PGSSO maintains an elite archive $\mathcal{E}_t = \{(\theta_i, s_i)\}_{i=1}^{e}$ where each configuration $\theta_i = (a_i, \phi_i)$ pairs an algorithm with its hyperparameters and $s_i = \mu_t(\theta_i)$ is its score. To remain responsive to drift, elites are ranked by a \textit{recency-weighted} score $s_i/(1+\Delta_i)$, where $\Delta_i$ is the number of windows since $\theta_i$ was last scored, so stale configurations are down-weighted in favour of recently effective ones. PGSSO balances exploration and exploitation through dynamic rate $\alpha_t$, computing statistical distributions over algorithm selections via frequencies and hyperparameters via means $m_j$ and variances $\sigma_j^2$, which together guide sampling of new configurations.

PGSSO generates new configurations through probabilistic sampling:

\begin{equation}
\theta_{\text{new}} = \begin{cases}
\text{Elite-Guided}(\mathcal{E}_t) & \text{with probability } (1-\alpha_t) \\
\text{Random}(\Theta) & \text{with probability } \alpha_t
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Elite-guided sampling first draws an algorithm with probability equal to its frequency in the elite archive, then samples that algorithm's numerical hyperparameters from a Gaussian fitted to the elites using it:

\begin{equation}
\phi_j \sim \mathcal{N}(m_j, \sigma_j^2)
\end{equation}

where the mean $m_j$ and variance $\sigma_j^2$ are computed over the elites that use the selected algorithm. Samples outside a hyperparameter's defined bounds are clipped to the boundary.

When the population exceeds size $K$, it is pruned by a farthest-first rule; the incumbent and top-scoring configurations are retained, then the most distant remaining configurations are added greedily to preserve diversity. The distance between two configurations is $1$ if their algorithms differ and the mean normalised per-hyperparameter distance otherwise. \textit{AutoDSC} emits a clustering for each instance using the incumbent $\theta^*_t$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:autodsc} details the complete operational steps.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{AutoDSC}
\label{alg:autodsc}
\KwIn{Stream $\mathcal{S} = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots\}$, $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$; window size $w$; population size $K$}
\KwOut{Cluster predictions $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^\infty$ using $\theta^*_t$}
$e_{\max} \gets \lfloor K/2 \rfloor$\;
$\mathcal{E}_0 \gets \emptyset$, $\alpha_0 \gets 0.5$\;
$\mathcal{A}_0 \gets \{\theta_i\}_{i=1}^K$ where $\theta_i \sim \text{Uniform}(\Theta)$\;
$W_0 \gets \emptyset$, $\theta^*_0 \gets \emptyset$\;
$\mu_{-1}(\theta^*_{-1}) \gets -\infty$\;
\ForEach{$x_t \in \mathcal{S}$}{
    $W_t \gets W_{t-1} \cup \{x_t\}$\;
    \ForEach{$\theta_i \in \mathcal{A}_t$}{
        $\ell_{i,t} \gets \textsc{PredictOne}(M_i, x_t)$\;
        \tcc{store assignment}
        $M_i \gets \textsc{LearnOne}(M_i, x_t)$\;
    }
    \If{$|W_t| = w$}{
        \ForEach{$\theta_i \in \mathcal{A}_t$}{
            $L_i \gets \{\ell_{i,\tau} : x_\tau \in W_t\}$\;
            $\mu_t(\theta_i) \gets \textsc{NormCVI}(W_t, L_i)$\;
        }
        $\mu_{\text{best}}^{(t)} \gets \max_i \mu_t(\theta_i)$\;
        $\alpha_{t+1} \gets \textsc{Adapt}(\alpha_t, \mu_{t-1}(\theta^*_{t-1}), \mu_{\text{best}}^{(t)})$\;
        $\mathcal{C}_{\text{new}} \gets \text{PGSSO}(\{(\theta_i, \mu_t(\theta_i))\}, \alpha_{t+1}, \Theta)$\;
        $\mathcal{A}_{t+1} \gets \textsc{SelectDiverse}(\mathcal{A}_t \cup \mathcal{C}_{\text{new}}, K)$\;
        $\theta^*_t \gets \arg\max_{\theta_i \in \mathcal{A}_{t+1}} \mu_t(\theta_i)$\;
        $W_t \gets \emptyset$\;
    }
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{PGSSO}
\label{alg:pgsso}
\KwIn{Current configurations with scores $\{(\theta_i, s_i)\}$; exploration rate $\alpha \in [0,1]$; parameter space $\Theta$}
\KwOut{New configuration set $\mathcal{C}_{\text{new}}$}
$\mathcal{E} \gets \textsc{UpdateElitePopulation}(\{(\theta_i, s_i)\}, e_{\max})$\;
\tcc{recency-weighted ranking}
$\mathcal{E} \gets \textsc{UpdateParameterDistributions}(\mathcal{E})$\;
\tcc{fit $\mathcal{N}(m_j,\sigma_j^2)$ per algorithm}
$\mathcal{C}_{\text{new}} \gets \emptyset$\;
\For{$j \gets 1$ \KwTo $n_{\text{gen}}$}{
    $u \gets \text{Uniform}(0,1)$\;
    \eIf{$u > \alpha$}{
        $\theta_{\text{new}} \gets \textsc{SampleFromElite}(\mathcal{E}, \Theta)$\;
    }{
        $\theta_{\text{new}} \gets \textsc{RandomSample}(\Theta)$\;
    }
    $\mathcal{C}_{\text{new}} \gets \mathcal{C}_{\text{new}} \cup \{\theta_{\text{new}}\}$\;
}
\Return $\mathcal{C}_{\text{new}}$\;
\end{algorithm}

The five real-world streams are public benchmarks with natural class structure and distribution change.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Insects}~\cite{souza2020challenges}: optical-sensor wing-beat features of flying insects, where the species labels and feature distribution shift with ambient temperature over the recording.
\item \textbf{KDD99}~\cite{dua2019uci}: network-connection records from the KDD Cup 1999 intrusion-detection task, labelled as normal traffic or one of several attack types.
\item \textbf{PenDigits}~\cite{dua2019uci}: pen-trajectory features of handwritten digits, with one cluster per digit $0$--$9$.
\item \textbf{Shuttle}~\cite{dua2019uci}: NASA Space Shuttle radiator sensor readings (Statlog), with highly imbalanced operational-state classes.
\item \textbf{Vehicle}~\cite{duarte2004vehicle}: high-dimensional acoustic and seismic features from a distributed wireless sensor network, labelled by vehicle type.
\end{itemize}

The five synthetic streams isolate specific drift mechanisms under controlled conditions, holding dimensionality ($d{=}5$) and cluster count ($k{=}5$) fixed. Four are Gaussian-blob streams where each cluster $c_i$ at time $t$ generates points $x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i(t),\,\sigma_i(t)^2 I)$, differing only in how $\mu_i(t)$ and $\sigma_i(t)$ evolve.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Expanding}: centroids are fixed ($\mu_i(t){=}\mu_i^0$); within-cluster standard deviation grows linearly, $\sigma_i(t) = \sigma_0 + \lambda t$, simulating continuous cluster expansion.
\item \textbf{Gradual}: each centroid translates at constant velocity along a fixed random direction, $\mu_i(t) = \mu_i^0 + v_i t$, producing smooth continuous drift throughout the stream.
\item \textbf{Merge-Split}: centroids move toward the global mean $\bar{\mu}$ during the first half of the stream and symmetrically re-separate in the second half, producing a single merge--split event.
\item \textbf{Sudden}: centroid positions jump instantaneously at three evenly spaced change points and remain stationary between them, producing four stationary segments separated by abrupt concept drift.
\item \textbf{Fast-RBF}: a MOA RandomRBF stream~\cite{bifet2010moa} with a high centroid speed parameter, generating continuously drifting clusters with complex shapes and overlapping boundaries.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

All experiments use the prequential protocol with multiple independent runs using random seeds ranges 1-100 to account for stochastic variability. Clustering quality is measured using ARI~\cite{hubert1985comparing} and CMM~\cite{kremer2011cmm}, where ground truth labels are used solely for evaluation, not training or model selection. We also record throughput (instances/s) and memory usage (MB) to assess computational efficiency. Statistical significance is assessed using the Friedman test followed by the Nemenyi post-hoc test~\cite{demvsar2006statistical} with $\alpha = 0.05$.

\subsection{Baseline Methods Selection}
We compare \textit{AutoDSC} against five baselines spanning three categories.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{AutoML:} \textit{ConfStream}~\cite{carnein2020confstream} and \textit{OACE}~\cite{el2023onlineautoclust}, established frameworks that optimise algorithm selection and hyperparameters online.
    \item \textbf{Rule-based:} \textit{MOStream}~\cite{wang2024mostream}, which reconfigures structural components by rule without searching the joint algorithm-and-hyperparameter space.
    \item \textbf{Baselines:} \textit{RandomSearch}~\cite{bergstra2012random}, which shares \textit{AutoDSC}'s framework structure but generates configurations by purely random sampling, and \textit{Default(Best)}~\cite{el2023onlineautoclust}, where one of the most commonly-used approaches by non-expert users is to try all possible clustering algorithms with their default hyperparameters and then select the model with the best performance.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}\label{sec:sensitivity}

Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity_study} reports \textit{AutoDSC}'s sensitivity to its two free hyperparameters, window size $w$ and population size $K$, averaged over all datasets. For window size, quality peaks at $w = 1000$, where smaller stages give insufficient data for reliable per-stage CVI evaluation and larger stages delay adaptation to drift and reduce throughput; the quality curve is thus unimodal with a clear optimum. For population size, both ARI and CMM rise steeply from small $K$ and saturate around $K \approx 15$, while throughput falls monotonically, so the default $K = 10$ sits at the knee of the quality-efficiency trade-off. Overall, \textit{AutoDSC} is robust within a broad range of both parameters and degrades gracefully outside it, with the default configuration ($w=1000$, $K=10$) providing the best balance between clustering quality and computational efficiency.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
\textit{AutoDSC} advances AutoML for streaming environments through several design choices. Its multi-objective scoring function, combining three normalised cluster-validity indices, prevents convergence to configurations that excel on one aspect while failing on others~\cite{arbelaitz2013extensive,liu2010understanding}, and PGSSO's adaptive exploration rate enables rapid recovery after concept drift while exploiting good configurations during stable periods. \textit{AutoDSC}'s clearest advantages are full autonomy, requiring no manual tuning or labels, and computational efficiency, together with statistically significant gains over untuned single algorithms and random search; its value is therefore best framed as delivering competitive quality fully automatically and at lower cost rather than as a uniform quality win. These contributions bridge traditional AutoML and the dynamic requirements of real-time streaming applications in IoT, sensor networks, and analytics platforms, where manual configuration is impractical~\cite{bahri2021data}.

Several limitations bound the scope of our claims. First, with only ten datasets, the quality advantage of \textit{AutoDSC} over the strongest AutoML competitors, ConfStream and OACE, is not statistically significant, although \textit{AutoDSC} attains the best mean rank and significantly outperforms untuned single algorithms and random search. Second, our evaluation targets tabular streams with numerical features, so extension to sparse text, multimodal, or image streams would require domain-specific base clusterers. Third, \textit{AutoDSC} still exposes two free hyperparameters, the window size and the population size, which require sensible defaults, although it remains stable across a broad range of both. Fourth, while PGSSO supports categorical hyperparameter sampling, the current portfolio uses only continuous and integer parameters, so algorithms with categorical choices would exercise this capability directly. Finally, configuration selection relies on internal validity indices computed without labels, so clustering quality is estimated rather than observed online, and a misleading internal score can temporarily affect selection between ground-truth evaluations.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion_and_future}
We presented \textit{AutoDSC}, a single-pass AutoML framework for data stream clustering that proposes configurations with PGSSO and selects among them with a label-free multi-objective measure, adapting implicitly as the stream drifts. Across ten streams, \textit{AutoDSC} attains the best mean rank on both ARI and CMM, with statistically significant gains over untuned single algorithms and random search, while remaining the fastest and most memory-efficient AutoML stream clusterer. These results demonstrate that fully autonomous, label-free algorithm selection and hyperparameter optimisation are both feasible and effective for evolving data streams, closing a gap that neither batch AutoML nor supervised stream AutoML addressed. Future work includes semi-supervised extensions and richer multi-objective optimisation objectives.

\begin{abstract}
We introduce AutoDSC (Automated Data Stream Clustering), an AutoML framework that selects a clustering algorithm and tunes its hyperparameters online, in a single pass over a drifting data stream and without access to labels. This matters because streams in IoT, network-monitoring, and sensor applications drift continuously, so no fixed algorithm or configuration stays optimal, yet clustering lacks a universal objective to optimize, which is why AutoML has transformed supervised learning but barely reached unsupervised streams. AutoDSC casts the task as an online combined algorithm selection and hyperparameter optimization (CASH) problem and solves it with Population-Guided Stochastic Search Optimization (PGSSO) and a label-free multi-objective cluster-validity criterion that adapts as the distribution shifts. We evaluate AutoDSC across ten real-world and synthetic streams against state-of-the-art stream-clustering and AutoML baselines. AutoDSC attains the best mean rank among all compared methods on both the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and the Cluster Mapping Measure (CMM) and the highest mean ARI overall, while remaining the most efficient of the AutoML stream clusterers at high throughput and low memory.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

On a data stream the data-generating distribution drifts over time, so no single clustering algorithm or hyperparameter setting stays optimal from start to finish; the right choice must itself change as the stream evolves. Automated machine learning (AutoML) was built to make exactly this kind of algorithm and hyperparameter choice automatically~\cite{hutter2019automated}, but almost entirely for batch supervised learning: clustering has no universally applicable objective to optimize~\cite{poulakis2024survey}, and streaming adds single-pass, bounded-memory, and concept-drift constraints that batch AutoML cannot meet~\cite{bifet2018machine}. We introduce AutoDSC (Automated Data Stream Clustering), an AutoML framework that selects the clustering algorithm and tunes its hyperparameters online, in a single pass over IoT, network, and sensor streams~\cite{yang2022iot}, and adapts continuously as the distribution drifts, all without labels.

Existing online AutoML frameworks~\cite{celik2023online,pmlr-v256-verma24a,pmlr-v139-wu21d,verma2025auto-reg} target supervised tasks, leaving unsupervised streaming clustering underserved. At present, ConfStream~\cite{carnein2020confstream} and OnlineAutoClust~\cite{el2023onlineautoclust}, which we abbreviate \emph{OACE} after its ensemble configuration, are the only established AutoML (CASH-style) solutions for stream clustering, and the recent MOStream~\cite{wang2024mostream} self-optimizes from a rule-based angle that reconfigures structural components rather than searching the joint algorithm--hyperparameter space. These solutions are limited: ConfStream relies on a single clustering metric, while OACE uses computationally intensive offline search, both ill-suited to real-time drifting streams~\cite{bahri2021data}. AutoDSC instead formalizes the streaming CASH problem and solves it with a single-pass guided search and a label-free multi-objective criterion, adapting to drift through continual per-window re-evaluation. As evidence we report ranks and quality across ten streams and an efficiency comparison; we do not claim a universally best clusterer, only the best automated selection among the algorithms in our portfolio. We evaluate AutoDSC against five baselines and make the following key contributions.

\begin{itemize}
\item We cast online stream clustering as a streaming combined algorithm selection and hyperparameter optimization (CASH) problem that jointly chooses the clustering algorithm and tunes its hyperparameters while the data distribution drifts, and we present AutoDSC, a single-pass AutoML framework that solves it autonomously without manual tuning or labels.

\item To propose new configurations, AutoDSC introduces Population-Guided Stochastic Search Optimization (PGSSO), which maintains a recency-weighted elite archive and an adaptively controlled exploration rate so that promising algorithm and hyperparameter settings are found quickly and the population recovers implicitly as the stream drifts.

\item To choose among configurations without ground-truth labels, AutoDSC scores each one with a normalized multi-objective criterion that combines three complementary internal cluster-validity indices, yielding robust selection where the single-metric criteria of prior systems fail.

\item Across ten real and synthetic streams, we show that AutoDSC attains the best mean rank on both the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)~\cite{hubert1985comparing} and CMM~\cite{kremer2011cmm} and the highest mean ARI among all methods while being the most efficient of the AutoML stream clusterers.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

We review the data stream clustering setting, how clustering quality is measured online, and the automated machine learning concepts that AutoDSC builds on.

\subsection{Data Stream Clustering}

A data stream is an unbounded, high-velocity sequence of instances that a learner must process online rather than as a stored dataset. Following Bifet et al.~\cite{bifet2018machine}, a stream learner should satisfy five requirements: (\textbf{R1}) process one instance at a time and inspect it at most once; (\textbf{R2}) use a bounded amount of time per instance; (\textbf{R3}) use a bounded amount of memory; (\textbf{R4}) be ready to produce an answer, such as a prediction, clustering, or set of patterns, at any time; and (\textbf{R5}) adapt to temporal changes in the data. These constraints rule out the multi-pass, full-data access that batch clustering and offline AutoML assume. Because the data-generating process evolves, streams also exhibit concept drift, meaning the distribution of the data, and for clustering the number, shape, and location of the clusters, changes over time, whether abruptly, gradually, or incrementally~\cite{bifet2018machine,aggarwal2013data}. A method that fixes its model or configuration in advance therefore degrades as the stream moves away from the regime it was tuned for, which is exactly what makes requirement R5 hard.

Classical stream clustering algorithms meet these requirements and fall into a few families distinguished by how they summarize the stream. The line begins with BIRCH~\cite{zhang1996birch}, whose clustering-feature tree first showed that a single-scan, bounded-memory summary could cluster very large datasets and seeded the micro-cluster summaries used by later stream methods. Micro-cluster summarization, pioneered by CluStream~\cite{aggarwal2003framework}, maintains pyramidal time-frame statistics for scalable analysis; density-based methods such as DenStream~\cite{Cao2006} and DBSTREAM~\cite{Hahsler2016} discover arbitrarily shaped clusters through core, potential, and outlier micro-clusters and a shared-density graph; and k-means-based methods maintain centroids online, where mini-batch optimization enables web-scale clustering at low latency~\cite{Sculley2010} and StreamKMeans~\cite{montiel2021river} replaces the k-medians step of stream local search~\cite{OCallaghan2002} with incremental k-means. These algorithms are effective once configured, but each fixes its algorithm and hyperparameters in advance, which motivates automating their selection. Quality is measured online with the interleaved test-then-train, or prequential, protocol~\cite{bifet2018machine}, in which each instance is used to test the model before it updates on it. As labels are unavailable during the stream, configuration choices rely on internal cluster validity indices (CVIs) such as the Silhouette, Davies--Bouldin, and Calinski--Harabasz scores, and since no single CVI is reliable across cluster shapes, densities, and counts, combining complementary indices is more robust than any one alone~\cite{arbelaitz2013extensive,liu2010understanding}; for label-based scoring, Kremer et al.~\cite{kremer2011cmm} introduced the Cluster Mapping Measure (CMM), which unlike classical external indices accounts for emerging, splitting, and moving clusters, so we evaluate with CMM alongside the chance-corrected Adjusted Rand Index (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

\subsection{Automated Machine Learning}

Automated Machine Learning removes the manual burden of algorithm selection and hyperparameter optimization, formalized as the CASH problem~\cite{hutter2019automated}. AutoML is mature for batch supervised learning, and the few streaming AutoML systems likewise target supervised tasks~\cite{celik2023online,pmlr-v256-verma24a}. For clustering, several batch AutoML systems automate the pipeline, such as AutoML4Clust~\cite{Tschechlov2021AutoML4Clust}, AutoCluster~\cite{Liu2021AutoCluster}, cSmartML~\cite{ElShawi2021cSmartML}, ML2DAC~\cite{TrederTschechlov2023ML2DAC}, TPE-AutoClust~\cite{ElShawi2025TPEAutoClust}, and PoAC~\cite{daSilva2024PoAC}, through meta-learning and pipeline composition, but all assume repeated full-data access and therefore cannot operate under the single-pass, bounded-memory, drifting conditions of a stream.

A few streaming approaches automate or self-optimize part of this choice (reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:relatedwork}); AutoDSC instead casts stream clustering as an online joint algorithm--hyperparameter search, which we formalize next.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

A data stream is an unbounded sequence $\mathcal{S} = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_t, \ldots\}$ in which each instance $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ arrives one at a time and must be processed in a single pass under bounded memory. We consume the stream in consecutive windows of fixed size $w$, writing $W$ for the window currently being processed, and the data-generating distribution may change from one window to the next (concept drift), so the best way to cluster the data is itself time-varying. Given a window $W$, a clustering algorithm groups its points by similarity without access to labels. Centroid-based methods, for instance, seek centroids that minimize the average distance from each point to its nearest centroid, while density- and micro-cluster-based methods optimize related criteria. No single algorithm or objective suits all data, so the appropriate choice varies across datasets and, under drift, across time. Because ground-truth labels are unavailable online, the quality of a clustering produced on $W$ must be judged by an internal cluster-validity score $q(\cdot) \in [0,1]$ (higher is better) computed from the data and the assignments alone, whereas external indices such as ARI and CMM use labels and are reserved for offline evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a portfolio of stream-clustering algorithms and $\Theta = \bigcup_{a \in \mathcal{A}} (\{a\} \times \Phi_a)$ the joint space of configurations $\theta = (a, \phi)$ that pair an algorithm $a$ with hyperparameters $\phi \in \Phi_a$. Running $\theta$ on the current window yields a clustering with label-free quality $q(\theta; W)$. The CASH problem for a stream is to track, at every window, the configuration
\begin{equation}
\theta^\star = \arg\max_{\theta \in \Theta}\; q(\theta; W),
\end{equation}
using only the data seen so far and bounded memory. As the distribution drifts, $\theta^\star$ changes over time, so a solver must continually re-evaluate and re-select configurations rather than commit to one. AutoDSC approximates this online optimum with a population-guided search and a label-free multi-objective instantiation of $q$, described next.

\section{Automated Data Stream Clustering}\label{sec:methodology}

We introduce Automated Data Stream Clustering (AutoDSC), a novel AutoML framework tailored for non-stationary data stream clustering. AutoDSC processes data in fixed-size windows. At the end of each window, AutoDSC evaluates clustering configurations using multiple normalized validation indices. The framework maintains a recency-weighted elite archive of high-performing configurations to guide future parameter exploration via PGSSO. It adaptively balances exploration and exploitation through dynamic rate adjustment based on performance feedback. Adaptation to distributional change is implicit, since, in each window, the population is re-evaluated and the incumbent re-selected, and a drop in clustering quality automatically raises the exploration rate so new configurations are sought for the new regime. Figure~\ref{fig:framework_overview} illustrates the high-level workflow of AutoDSC, while Algorithm~\ref{alg:autodsc} details the complete operational steps.

To solve the online CASH problem of Section~\ref{sec:problem}, AutoDSC maintains a configuration population $\mathcal{A}_t \subseteq \Theta$ of clustering algorithms with different hyperparameter settings, fills the current window $W_t$ as instances arrive, and serves real-time predictions from the best-performing (incumbent) configuration. As each new data point arrives, every configuration in the population updates incrementally on it with a single \textsc{LearnOne} step, retaining the per-point cluster assignment for later scoring. When the window reaches capacity, the framework initiates evaluation and optimization.

When the window fills, AutoDSC evaluates clustering quality using three normalized clustering validation indices, namely the Normalized Silhouette Coefficient $\text{NSC} = (1+\text{SC})/2$, the Normalized Davies-Bouldin Index $\text{NDBI} = 1-\tanh(\text{DBI})$, and the Normalized Calinski-Harabasz Index $\text{NCHI} = \tanh(\log(1+\text{CHI})/10)$, ensuring all metrics are bounded to $[0,1]$ where higher values indicate better clustering quality. Each configuration is scored on the entire window (the per-point cluster assignments produced during \textsc{LearnOne} are retained for this purpose), so no sub-sampling is required. The expected clustering quality for configuration $\theta$ at time $t$ is:

\begin{equation}
\mu_t(\theta) = \frac{1}{3}[\text{NSC} + \text{NDBI} + \text{NCHI}]
\end{equation}

AutoDSC adjusts exploration based on the change in the best score between consecutive windows, $\Delta\mu_t = \mu_t(\theta^*_t) - \mu_{t-1}(\theta^*_{t-1})$. The exploration rate then adapts as:

\begin{equation}
\alpha_{t+1} = \begin{cases}
\max(0.1,\; \alpha_t \cdot (1 - \beta_t)) & \text{if } \Delta \mu_t > 0 \\
\min(1.0,\; \alpha_t \cdot (1 + \beta_t)) & \text{if } \Delta \mu_t \leq 0
\end{cases}
\quad \beta_t = \min(\gamma, |r_t|)
\end{equation}

where $r_t = \Delta\mu_t / |\mu_{t-1}(\theta^*_{t-1})|$ is the relative improvement and $\beta_t$ is the resulting step, capped at $\gamma = 0.5$ for stability. This parameter-free mechanism decreases exploration when performance improves (favoring exploitation) and increases it when performance stagnates or drops, as happens under concept drift, encouraging broader search to recover.

The framework employs \textbf{PGSSO} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:pgsso}) to jointly optimize algorithm selection and hyperparameter tuning. PGSSO maintains an elite archive $\mathcal{E}_t = \{(\theta_i, s_i)\}_{i=1}^{e}$ where each configuration $\theta_i = (a_i, \phi_i)$ pairs an algorithm choice with its hyperparameters and $s_i = \mu_t(\theta_i)$ is its score. To remain responsive to drift, elites are ranked by a \emph{recency-weighted} score $s_i/(1+\Delta_i)$, where $\Delta_i$ is the number of windows since $\theta_i$ was last scored, so stale high-scoring configurations are down-weighted in favor of recently effective ones. By tracking which algorithm-hyperparameter combinations perform best, PGSSO naturally learns both which algorithms suit current data characteristics and their optimal settings. The elite population encodes this joint preference, where frequently appearing algorithms indicate superior algorithmic choices, while their parameter distributions reveal effective settings. PGSSO adaptively balances exploration and exploitation through dynamic rate $\alpha_t$, computing statistical distributions over both algorithm selections (via frequencies) and their parameters (via means $m_j$ and variances $\sigma_j^2$), which together guide sampling of new promising algorithm-hyperparameter configurations.

PGSSO generates new configurations through probabilistic sampling:

\begin{equation}
\theta_{\text{new}} = \begin{cases}
\text{Elite-Guided}(\mathcal{E}_t) & \text{with probability } (1-\alpha_t) \\
\text{Random}(\Theta) & \text{with probability } \alpha_t
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Elite-guided sampling first draws an algorithm with probability equal to its frequency in the elite archive, then samples that algorithm's numerical hyperparameters from a Gaussian fitted to the elites using it:

\begin{equation}
\phi_j \sim \mathcal{N}(m_j, \sigma_j^2)
\end{equation}

where the mean $m_j$ and variance $\sigma_j^2$ are computed over the elites that use the selected algorithm. Categorical hyperparameters use the same frequency-based sampling.

When the population exceeds size $K$, it is pruned by a \emph{farthest-first} rule: keep the incumbent and the top-scoring configurations, then greedily add whichever remaining configuration is most distant from those already kept. The distance between two configurations is $1$ if their algorithms differ and the mean normalized per-hyperparameter distance otherwise, preserving diversity without a fixed acceptance threshold.

Throughout, AutoDSC emits a clustering for each instance using the incumbent $\theta^*_t$, the highest-scoring configuration in the current population.

All remaining constants are held fixed across every experiment. The exploration rate is clipped to $[0.1, 1.0]$ with a per-step cap $\gamma = 0.5$, the elite archive holds $e_{\max} = \lfloor K/2 \rfloor$ configurations, and each window PGSSO proposes $n_{\text{gen}} = \max(3, \lfloor K/4 \rfloor)$ new candidates (three at the default $K=10$) that replace the lowest-ranked members so the population size stays at $K$. The Calinski--Harabasz score is rescaled by a fixed factor of $10$ to bound NCHI to $[0,1]$. The sensitivity study (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}) varies only $K$ and $w$ and holds all other constants at these defaults.

\subsection{Computational Complexity}

The framework's computational complexity scales efficiently with population size and window parameters. Per-instance processing requires $O(K \cdot C)$ operations where $K = |\mathcal{A}_t|$ and $C$ denotes average algorithm complexity. Window-end evaluation involves $O(K \cdot w \cdot d)$ operations to score each configuration on the full window. Total per-window complexity is $O(K \cdot w \cdot d + K^2)$, where $K^2$ accounts for diversity-based population management. Crucially, $K$ is fixed, so per-point cost does not grow with stream length. Space complexity is $O((K + e_{\max}) \cdot p + w \cdot d)$, where $p$ is the number of parameters stored per configuration.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{AutoDSC}
\label{alg:autodsc}
\KwIn{Stream $\mathcal{S} = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots\}$, $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$; window size $w$; population size $K$}
\KwOut{Cluster predictions $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^\infty$ using $\theta^*_t$}
$e_{\max} \gets \lfloor K/2 \rfloor$\;
$\mathcal{E}_0 \gets \emptyset$, $\alpha_0 \gets 0.5$\;
$\mathcal{A}_0 \gets \{\theta_i\}_{i=1}^K$ where $\theta_i \sim \text{Uniform}(\Theta)$\;
$W_0 \gets \emptyset$, $\theta^*_0 \gets \emptyset$\;
$\mu_{-1}(\theta^*_{-1}) \gets -\infty$\;
\ForEach{$x_t \in \mathcal{S}$}{
    $W_t \gets W_{t-1} \cup \{x_t\}$\;
    \ForEach{$\theta_i \in \mathcal{A}_t$}{
        $\ell_{i,t} \gets \textsc{PredictOne}(M_i, x_t)$ \tcp*{store assignment}
        $M_i \gets \textsc{LearnOne}(M_i, x_t)$\;
    }
    \If{$|W_t| = w$}{
        \ForEach{$\theta_i \in \mathcal{A}_t$}{
            $L_i \gets \{\ell_{i,\tau} : x_\tau \in W_t\}$\;
            $\mu_t(\theta_i) \gets \textsc{NormCVI}(W_t, L_i)$\;
        }
        $\mu_{\text{best}}^{(t)} \gets \max_i \mu_t(\theta_i)$\;
        $\alpha_{t+1} \gets \textsc{Adapt}(\alpha_t, \mu_{t-1}(\theta^*_{t-1}), \mu_{\text{best}}^{(t)})$\;
        $\mathcal{C}_{\text{new}} \gets \text{PGSSO}(\{(\theta_i, \mu_t(\theta_i))\}, \alpha_{t+1}, \Theta)$\;
        $\mathcal{A}_{t+1} \gets \textsc{SelectDiverse}(\mathcal{A}_t \cup \mathcal{C}_{\text{new}}, K)$\;
        $\theta^*_t \gets \arg\max_{\theta_i \in \mathcal{A}_{t+1}} \mu_t(\theta_i)$\;
        $W_t \gets \emptyset$\;
    }
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{PGSSO}
\label{alg:pgsso}
\KwIn{Current configurations with scores $\{(\theta_i, s_i)\}$; exploration rate $\alpha \in [0,1]$; parameter space $\Theta$}
\KwOut{New configuration set $\mathcal{C}_{\text{new}}$}
$\mathcal{E} \gets \textsc{UpdateElitePopulation}(\{(\theta_i, s_i)\}, e_{\max})$\;
$\mathcal{E} \gets \textsc{UpdateParameterDistributions}(\mathcal{E})$\;
$\mathcal{C}_{\text{new}} \gets \emptyset$\;
\For{$j \gets 1$ \KwTo $n_{\text{gen}}$}{
    $u \gets \text{Uniform}(0,1)$\;
    \eIf{$u > \alpha$}{
        $\theta_{\text{new}} \gets \textsc{SampleFromElite}(\mathcal{E}, \Theta)$\;
    }{
        $\theta_{\text{new}} \gets \textsc{RandomSample}(\Theta)$\;
    }
    $\mathcal{C}_{\text{new}} \gets \mathcal{C}_{\text{new}} \cup \{\theta_{\text{new}}\}$\;
}
\Return $\mathcal{C}_{\text{new}}$\;
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

The five real-world streams are public benchmarks with natural class structure and distribution change.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Insects}~\cite{souza2020challenges}: optical-sensor wing-beat features of flying insects, where the species labels and feature distribution shift with ambient temperature over the recording.
\item \textbf{KDD99}~\cite{dua2019uci}: network-connection records from the KDD Cup 1999 intrusion-detection task, labelled as normal traffic or one of several attack types.
\item \textbf{PenDigits}~\cite{dua2019uci}: pen-trajectory features of handwritten digits, with one cluster per digit $0$--$9$.
\item \textbf{Shuttle}~\cite{dua2019uci}: NASA Space Shuttle radiator sensor readings (Statlog), with highly imbalanced operational-state classes.
\item \textbf{Vehicle}~\cite{duarte2004vehicle}: high-dimensional acoustic and seismic features from a distributed wireless sensor network, labelled by vehicle type.
\end{itemize}

The five synthetic streams are produced by our reproducible generators (released with the code) and let us control the drift mechanism while holding dimensionality and cluster count fixed. Four are five-cluster Gaussian-blob streams in $[0,1]^5$ that differ only in the injected drift, and one is a MOA-style RandomRBF stream~\cite{bifet2010moa}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Expanding}: cluster centroids stay fixed while the within-cluster variance grows steadily over the stream.
\item \textbf{Gradual}: centroids translate smoothly at constant speed along fixed random directions.
\item \textbf{Merge-Split}: clusters converge toward the global mean at mid-stream and then re-separate.
\item \textbf{Sudden}: centroids jump abruptly at three change points, holding stationary between them.
\item \textbf{Fast-RBF}: a RandomRBF stream whose class centroids drift rapidly across the feature space.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol} All experiments use the prequential protocol (Section~\ref{sec:background}) with 10 independent runs using random seeds 1-10 to account for stochastic variability. Clustering quality is measured using ARI and CMM~\cite{kremer2011cmm}, where ground truth labels are used solely for evaluation, not training or model selection. ARI is the chance-corrected form~\cite{hubert1985comparing} of the Rand index~\cite{rand1971objective} that counts agreements between the predicted clustering and the ground-truth partition over all pairs of points and adjusts the count for the agreement expected at random, yielding $1$ for a perfect match, $0$ for random labelling, and negative values for worse-than-random partitions. We also record throughput (instances/s) and peak memory usage (MB) to assess computational efficiency. Statistical significance is assessed using the Friedman test followed by the Nemenyi post-hoc test~\cite{demvsar2006statistical} with $\alpha = 0.05$.

\subsection{Baseline Methods}
We compare AutoDSC against five baselines spanning three categories. (i) \emph{AutoML stream-clustering methods}: \textit{ConfStream}~\cite{carnein2020confstream} and \textit{OACE}~\cite{el2023onlineautoclust}, established frameworks that optimize hyperparameters online. (ii) The \emph{rule-based adaptive} method \textit{MOStream}~\cite{wang2024mostream}. (iii) Two \emph{baselines}: \textit{RandomSearch}~\cite{bergstra2012random}, which shares AutoDSC's framework structure but generates configurations by purely random sampling, a direct comparison to our guided optimization, and the \textit{Default(Best)} baseline of OACE~\cite{el2023onlineautoclust}, which captures that ``one of the most commonly-used approaches by non-expert users is to try all possible clustering algorithms with their default hyperparameters and then select the model with the best performance.'' Following this, we run the four River fixed algorithms (CluStream, DenStream, DBSTREAM, and StreamKMeans) with their default settings and report the best-performing model per dataset.

\subsection{Implementation and Configuration}
Unless otherwise stated, AutoDSC uses its default configuration of population size $K=10$ and window size $w=1000$, with the exploration rate adapting automatically from an initial value of $0.5$ (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} analyzes sensitivity to $K$ and $w$). The AutoML baselines search over the same algorithm portfolio and hyperparameter ranges specified in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}, where the window-dependent ranges (\texttt{time\_window} and \texttt{chunk\_size}) are listed for the default window size $w=1000$; the single fixed algorithms use their library defaults. To ensure a fair comparison, all AutoML baselines receive the same window size and per-window evaluation budget as AutoDSC, so differences reflect the search and selection strategy rather than the compute allotment; other baseline-specific parameters remain as reported in their original papers.

\subsection{Supplementary Analyses}\label{sec:supplementary}

\textbf{Label-free selection is sound.} AutoDSC selects the incumbent each window with a label-free internal criterion, so its value hinges on that criterion tracking the unobserved external quality. On a real (PenDigits) and a drifting synthetic (Sudden) stream we bucket each window into terciles of the incumbent's internal multi-CVI score and measure the mean external ARI per bucket (Figure~\ref{fig:supplementary}a). On PenDigits, ARI rises monotonically with the internal score, from $0.33$ in the lowest tercile to $0.48$ in the highest (Spearman $\rho=0.43$); on Sudden nearly every window already attains ARI~$\approx1.0$, leaving little headroom to separate ($\rho=0.17$). The label-free score is thus a sound, if imperfect, basis for selection, consistent with the limitation noted in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. More strikingly, although AutoDSC never observes the number of clusters, the incumbent's mean per-window cluster count matches the ground truth almost exactly ($10.2$ vs.\ $10$ on PenDigits, $5.0$ vs.\ $5$ on Sudden), addressing the long-standing problem of choosing $k$ online.

\textbf{The search overhead is negligible.} Decomposing AutoDSC's per-window wall-clock into base-clusterer training/prediction, multi-CVI scoring, and the PGSSO search itself (Figure~\ref{fig:supplementary}b) reveals three well-separated tiers: training the $K$ population members dominates ($\approx1.0$--$1.3$\,s/window), multi-CVI scoring is an order of magnitude smaller ($\approx0.1$\,s), and the AutoML search machinery is three orders smaller still ($\approx1$\,ms, under $0.1\%$ of the total). The price of automation is thus dwarfed by the clustering it automates, explaining AutoDSC's competitive throughput against the offline optimization of OACE.

\textbf{Quality is stable across dimensionality.} On a controlled five-cluster blob stream with feature dimensionality growing from $5$ to $80$ (Figure~\ref{fig:dimscaling}), clustering quality stays high and even improves slightly (ARI $0.91\rightarrow0.97$) while throughput degrades only gracefully ($620\rightarrow376$ instances/s), with no curse-of-dimensionality collapse, indicating that AutoDSC's search remains effective as dimensionality increases.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}\label{sec:sensitivity}

We analyze AutoDSC's sensitivity to its two free hyperparameters, window size $w$ and population size $K$, averaged over all datasets ($\times10$ seeds), jointly reporting clustering quality (ARI) and throughput (Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity_study}). Window size shows a clear quality optimum at $w = 1000$, where smaller windows give insufficient data for reliable per-window CVI evaluation, while larger windows delay adaptation to drift and reduce throughput. Population size exhibits a quality plateau, where ARI rises steeply from $K=5$ and saturates by $K\approx15$, while throughput falls monotonically as $K$ grows. The knee of this trade-off is at $K=10$, which attains near-peak quality at substantially higher throughput, justifying it as the default. Overall, AutoDSC is stable within reasonable ranges and degrades gracefully outside them, with the default configuration ($w=1000$, $K=10$) sitting at the quality/efficiency sweet spot of both parameters.

\section{Prior Work}\label{sec:relatedwork}

\textbf{AutoML for clustering.} Batch AutoML systems---AutoML4Clust, AutoCluster, cSmartML, ML2DAC, TPE-AutoClust, and PoAC~\cite{Tschechlov2021AutoML4Clust,Liu2021AutoCluster,ElShawi2021cSmartML,TrederTschechlov2023ML2DAC,ElShawi2025TPEAutoClust,daSilva2024PoAC}---automate clustering-pipeline design through meta-learning, Bayesian optimization, and pipeline composition, but all assume repeated full-data access and an offline tuning budget, so none meets the single-pass, bounded-memory, drifting constraints of a stream.

\textbf{Online AutoML.} Streaming AutoML frameworks~\cite{celik2023online,pmlr-v256-verma24a,pmlr-v139-wu21d,verma2025auto-reg} adapt configurations on the fly but target \emph{supervised} tasks, where a label-based loss drives selection---unavailable in unsupervised stream clustering.

\textbf{Automated stream clustering.} The closest works are ConfStream~\cite{carnein2020confstream}, OACE~\cite{el2023onlineautoclust}, and MOStream~\cite{wang2024mostream}. ConfStream maintains an ensemble of configurations scored by a single Silhouette metric; OACE combines clustering ensembles with Tree-structured Parzen Estimator Bayesian optimization but relies on computationally heavy offline tuning that is sensitive to its time budget; and MOStream self-optimizes from a rule-based angle, reconfiguring four structural design dimensions rather than searching the joint algorithm--hyperparameter space, descending from the modular Benne algorithm of the SESAME study~\cite{wang2023sesame}. AutoDSC differs on the three axes that matter for a stream clusterer: it searches the \emph{joint} algorithm--hyperparameter space (unlike MOStream), with a single-pass guided population search (unlike OACE's offline optimization), and selects using a \emph{multi-objective} label-free criterion (unlike ConfStream's single metric), adapting to drift through continual per-window re-evaluation.

\textbf{Classical stream clustering.} All of these ultimately configure the classical single-algorithm stream clusterers of Section~\ref{sec:background} (BIRCH, CluStream, DenStream, DBSTREAM, StreamKMeans), each fixed in advance; AutoDSC automates that choice and re-makes it as the stream drifts.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
AutoDSC advances AutoML for streaming environments through several design choices. Its multi-objective criterion, combining three normalized cluster-validity indices, prevents convergence to configurations that excel on one aspect while failing catastrophically on others~\cite{arbelaitz2013extensive,liu2010understanding}, and PGSSO's adaptive exploration rate enables rapid recovery after concept drift while exploiting good configurations during stable periods. We emphasize that AutoDSC's clearest advantages are its full autonomy, requiring no manual tuning or labels, and its efficiency, together with statistically significant gains over untuned single algorithms and random search; we therefore frame its value as delivering competitive quality fully automatically and at lower cost rather than as a uniform quality win (scope and significance caveats are detailed below). These contributions bridge traditional AutoML and the dynamic requirements of real-time streaming applications in IoT, sensor networks, and analytics platforms, where manual configuration is impractical~\cite{bahri2021data}.

Several limitations bound the scope of our claims. First, with only ten datasets the quality advantage of AutoDSC over the strongest AutoML competitors (ConfStream and OACE) is not statistically significant, although AutoDSC attains the best mean rank and significantly outperforms untuned single algorithms and random search. Second, our evaluation targets tabular streams with numerical and categorical features, so extension to sparse text, multi-modal, or image streams would require domain-specific base clusterers. Third, AutoDSC still exposes two free hyperparameters, the window size and the population size, which require sensible defaults, although it remains stable across a broad range of both. Finally, configuration selection relies on internal validity indices computed without labels, so online clustering quality is estimated rather than observed, and a misleading internal score can temporarily mislead selection between ground-truth evaluations.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion_and_future}

We presented AutoDSC, a single-pass AutoML framework for data stream clustering that proposes configurations with a population-guided search over a recency-weighted elite archive and selects among them with a label-free multi-objective criterion, adapting implicitly as the stream drifts. Across ten streams it attains the best mean rank on both ARI and CMM and the highest mean ARI of all methods---significantly better than untuned single algorithms---while being the fastest and most memory-efficient AutoML stream clusterer. Future work includes semi-supervised and multi-task extensions and richer multi-objective optimization for dynamic settings.
